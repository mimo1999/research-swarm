# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists as a Neuroprotective, Disease-Modifying Therapy in Parkinson's Disease

## Summary

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists — a drug class approved for type 2 diabetes and obesity — are under active investigation as a repurposed, disease-modifying treatment for Parkinson's disease (PD). The rationale rests on preclinical evidence that GLP-1 receptor stimulation is anti-inflammatory, neurotrophic, and neuroprotective ([Kopp et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phrs.2022.106550)), and this rationale has now been tested in two independent phase 2 clinical trials with converging positive results.

## Clinical Trial Evidence

The first randomized trial ([Athauda et al., *Lancet*, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31585-4)) gave 62 patients with moderate PD once-weekly subcutaneous **exenatide** or placebo for 48 weeks. At 60 weeks (after a 12-week washout), off-medication motor scores (MDS-UPDRS part III) had improved by 1.0 point with exenatide versus worsening by 2.1 points with placebo — an adjusted difference of **-3.5 points (95% CI -6.7 to -0.3, p=0.0318)**. Because the effect persisted through washout, the authors suggested a genuine disease-modifying effect rather than a purely symptomatic one, though they stopped short of confirming this.

A second, larger trial ([Meissner et al., *NEJM*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2312323), the LIXIPARK trial) randomized 156 patients with early PD to daily **lixisenatide** or placebo for 12 months. The lixisenatide group's motor scores were essentially stable (-0.04 points) while the placebo group worsened (+3.04 points) — a between-group difference of **3.08 points (95% CI 0.86–5.30, p=0.007)**. Gastrointestinal side effects were common: 46% nausea, 13% vomiting.

Both trials converge on the same primary endpoint (MDS-UPDRS III) with similar-magnitude effects (~3–3.5 points) despite different molecules, dose schedules, and trial sites — a meaningful cross-validation signal. Both papers are also explicit that these are phase 2 results only, and that larger, longer trials are required before any change to clinical practice ([De Giorgi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2024-335593)).

## Mechanism

Early mechanistic framing centered on neuroinflammation and insulin-resistance pathways shared between type 2 diabetes and neurodegeneration ([Kopp et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phrs.2022.106550)). A 2025 review broadens this to include brain energy homeostasis, synaptic function, proteostasis (protein aggregate clearance), and blood-brain barrier dynamics as candidate mechanisms ([De Giorgi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp-2024-335593)). Most recently, a preclinical study of the newer dual GLP-1/GIP agonist **tirzepatide** in an MPTP-induced mouse model identified a specific, testable mechanism: tirzepatide reduced pathological mitochondrial fission (via Drp1 inhibition) and promoted mitophagy (via the Pink1/Parkin pathway), preserving dopaminergic neurons and ATP production — at one-third the dose, effects were statistically comparable to semaglutide and levodopa ([Tian et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intimp.2025.115443)). The same study found GLP-1 and GIP receptor expression reduced in dopaminergic neurons from PD patients, offering a disease-relevance anchor for the mouse-model findings.

## Open Questions

The literature is consistent in flagging: (1) uncertain brain penetrance of these peptide drugs across the blood-brain barrier, (2) the absence of validated response biomarkers to identify which patients benefit, (3) a consistent GI tolerability burden, and (4) the lack of long-term (multi-year) efficacy and safety data. No trial to date is powered or designed to distinguish a true disease-modifying effect from a durable symptomatic one.
